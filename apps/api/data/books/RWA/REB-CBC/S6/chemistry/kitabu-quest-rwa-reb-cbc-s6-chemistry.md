## How To Use This Book

This book helps S6 learners study Chemistry one teachable topic at a time.

1. Start with the inquiry question and say what you already know.
2. Read the Learn section slowly and mark new words.
3. Try the worked example before reading the full solution.
4. Complete the activity and practice tasks.
5. Correct your answer using the success criteria.
6. Ask for support when your answer is still unclear.

Subject method: Use safe observations, simple models, data tables, and evidence-based explanations.

## Learning Skills In This Book

Each topic builds knowledge, skill, values, communication, collaboration, critical thinking, creativity, digital awareness, and self-confidence.

Study actively: speak answers aloud, draw when useful, show your working, cite evidence, use local examples, and correct mistakes after feedback.

A strong answer is specific. It names the idea, gives evidence or method, applies it to the task, and checks whether it answers the question.

## Benzene: Learn

Benzene introduces benzene as an aromatic hydrocarbon with a special ring structure.

Core idea:
- Benzene has six carbon atoms and six hydrogen atoms in a ring.
- Its bonding is represented by alternating double bonds or a circle inside the ring to show delocalised electrons.
- Benzene reactions are often substitution reactions rather than simple addition reactions.
- Benzene and many organic solvents must be handled only through teacher-approved demonstrations because safety matters.
- Structure, formula, properties, and safe-use rules should be connected, not memorised separately.

## Benzene: Worked Example

Worked example for Benzene: Interpret a benzene ring diagram.

1. Count six carbon positions in the ring.
2. Add one hydrogen attached to each carbon to give C6H6.
3. Explain that the ring can be drawn with alternating double bonds or a circle showing delocalised electrons.
4. State one reaction idea: benzene often undergoes substitution reactions.
5. Add one safety rule for aromatic compounds and solvents.

Conclusion: benzene's ring structure explains why its reactions differ from many alkenes.

## Radioactivity: Learn

Radioactivity explains radioactivity as the process where unstable nuclei emit radiation to become more stable.

Core idea:
- Alpha, beta, and gamma radiation differ in charge, mass, penetration, and shielding.
- Half-life is the time taken for half of a radioactive sample or count rate to remain.
- Radioactivity can be useful in medicine, industry, agriculture, dating, and research when controlled safely.
- Radiation can also damage living tissue, so distance, shielding, time limits, and trained supervision are essential.
- Learners should interpret simple count-rate data and avoid unsafe experiments.

## Radioactivity: Worked Example

Worked example for Radioactivity: Use half-life data.

A sample has a count rate of 80 counts per minute. Its half-life is 5 minutes.

1. After 5 minutes, half remains: 40 counts per minute.
2. After 10 minutes, half of 40 remains: 20 counts per minute.
3. After 15 minutes, half of 20 remains: 10 counts per minute.
4. Shielding and distance must be controlled by trained adults.

Answer: after 15 minutes, the count rate is 10 counts per minute.
