CBC vs CBE: what actually changed for Kenyan learners

Kitabu AI team · Updated 4 July 2026

CBE is the new official name for Kenya’s competency-based education system. The day-to-day approach your child experiences did not change — the framework around it was reorganised, mainly at Senior School. Here is exactly what changed, and what did not.

The short answer

CBE is the new official name for Kenya’s competency-based education system. If your child is in Grade 4 through Grade 10 today, the day-to-day experience — a teacher assessing understanding continuously, lessons organised around competencies rather than memorising for one final exam — has not changed. What changed is the framework around it: following recommendations from the Presidential Working Party on Education Reform, the system once called CBC is now officially referred to as CBE, with Senior School reorganised around three learning pathways.

In three sentences: CBC was renamed CBE. The classroom approach your child lives through every day is the same competency-based approach it always was. The part that actually reorganised is Senior School, Grade 10 and above, where learners now choose one of three pathways.

This matters because search results, WhatsApp groups, and even some school circulars currently use "CBC" and "CBE" as if they were two different systems in competition with each other. They are not. One is the earlier name; the other is the current one. A parent trying to make sense of a confusing news headline or an anxious parenting group thread can treat this page as the plain, dated answer: nothing about your child’s classroom experience changed overnight, and nothing requires an urgent decision today unless your child is approaching Grade 9.

A plain timeline

Understanding CBC vs CBE is easiest as a sequence of events, not a sudden switch:

  1. 2017 — CBC introduced. The Competency Based Curriculum began progressively replacing the old 8-4-4 system, shifting the emphasis from memorisation toward demonstrated competencies, continuous assessment, and applied learning.
  2. Presidential Working Party on Education Reform. A national review process examined how the curriculum was working in practice and made recommendations for how it should be structured going forward, particularly around Senior School pathways.
  3. Renaming to CBE, with reorganised pathways. Following those recommendations, the framework became officially known as Competency Based Education (CBE) — the same competency approach, now organised around three Senior School pathways: STEM, Social Sciences, and Arts & Sports Science.
  4. January 2026 — first Senior School Grade 10 cohort. The first group of learners entered Senior School under the reorganised structure, choosing a pathway as they moved into Grade 10.

Laid out this way, the sequence is ordinary policy evolution, not an abrupt reversal: a curriculum introduced in 2017, reviewed nationally through a formal process years later, and then renamed and reorganised at its far end — Senior School — as the first cohort reached that stage in January 2026. Nothing in that sequence removed or replaced the competency-based approach itself.

The 2-6-3-3-3 structure, like a map of your child’s journey

Both CBC and CBE sit inside the same overall structure, written as 2-6-3-3-3. Reading it as a map of a learner’s journey through the system:

  • 2 years of pre-primary — early learning before formal schooling begins.
  • 6 years of primary — Grade 1 through Grade 6, ending with the KPSEA assessment.
  • 3 years of junior school — Grade 7 through Grade 9, ending with the KJSEA assessment, which determines Senior School pathway placement.
  • 3 years of senior school — Grade 10 through Grade 12, organised into the STEM, Social Sciences, or Arts & Sports Science pathway a learner has chosen.
  • 3 years (or more) of tertiary education — university, college, or technical and vocational training beyond Grade 12.

A Grade 6 learner today is finishing primary and about to sit KPSEA. A Grade 9 learner is finishing junior school and about to sit KJSEA, which feeds directly into which Senior School pathway they’re placed into. Seen this way, CBC and CBE describe the same map — the renaming happened around Grade 10 and above, at the far end of the journey most Grade 4–10 families are on today.

It helps to place your own child on that map before reading further. A Grade 4 or 5 learner is in the middle of primary school, years away from any pathway decision — the renaming has essentially no practical effect on their week. A Grade 7 or 8 learner is inside junior school, building toward KJSEA and the placement score it feeds. A Grade 9 learner is at the most consequential point on the map right now, sitting the assessment that determines which Senior School pathway they enter. Knowing where your child sits on the 2-6-3-3-3 map tells you how much of this renaming actually requires your attention this year.

What stayed the same

This is the part most parents actually need to hear: for a Grade 4–10 learner, very little about daily schoolwork changed with the CBE renaming.

  • Competencies over cramming. The core philosophy — assessing whether a learner can apply an idea, not just recall it for one exam day — is unchanged.
  • Continuous assessment. Learning is still tracked through the term, not judged solely on a single final paper.
  • Learning areas. The subjects and learning areas a child studies day to day continue under the same structure.
  • CBC-aligned materials remain valid. Textbooks, revision guides, and learning tools built around CBC strands are still aligned to what a learner is taught and assessed on — the renaming did not invalidate them.

What actually changed

Two things changed, and both matter most from Grade 9 upward:

  • The name. Competency Based Curriculum (CBC) is now officially Competency Based Education (CBE). Both names describe the same underlying system.
  • The Senior School pathway organisation. Grade 10 to 12 is now organised into three named pathways — STEM, Social Sciences, and Arts & Sports Science — rather than a single undifferentiated Senior School track. A learner’s KJSEA results and school-based assessments feed into which pathway they’re placed into.

Notice what is absent from that list: there is no mention of a different way of teaching Grade 5 fractions, no new set of learning areas for a Grade 7 learner, and no change to how a Grade 4 class is assessed day to day. The reorganisation is concentrated at the point where a learner moves from junior school into Senior School — everything before that point continues under the same competency-based approach it always has.

The assessments, in one place

Two national checkpoints run by KNEC matter most for Grade 4–10 families right now:

  • KPSEA (Kenya Primary School Education Assessment), Grade 6. Sat at the end of primary school. It is a monitoring assessment, not a ranked pass/fail exam like the old KCPE, and every learner proceeds to Grade 7 regardless of the result. It covers five papers — Mathematics, English, Kiswahili/Kenyan Sign Language, Integrated Science, and Creative Arts & Social Studies — and contributes 20% to the eventual junior-school placement score. Our KPSEA Grade 6 revision guide covers the full breakdown.
  • KJSEA (Kenya Junior School Education Assessment), Grade 9. Sat at the end of junior school, with the 2026 exam window running 26 October to 20 November 2026 — it was first sat in November 2025. Results are not ranked school-by-school like the old KCPE. The placement score for Senior School is calculated as 20% KPSEA, 20% school-based assessments across Grades 7–8, and 60% the KJSEA exam itself, across nine learning areas. That placement takes effect from 2027 for the 2026 cohort. Our KJSEA Grade 9 parent’s guide covers dates, scoring, and a term-by-term plan.

Both assessments are set out in KNEC’s own guidelines at knec.ac.ke, and the curriculum designs behind them are published by KICD at kicd.ac.ke.

What parents should actually do about it

Mostly: nothing dramatic. The renaming to CBE is not a reason to change how you support a Grade 4–8 learner today — the same routines that helped under "CBC" still apply under "CBE," because it is the same system.

Two things are worth doing deliberately:

  1. Understand the pathway decision coming in Grade 9. If your child is approaching junior school, know that KJSEA results and Grades 7–8 school-based assessments will feed into which Senior School pathway — STEM, Social Sciences, or Arts & Sports Science — they’re placed into. It is worth a calm conversation about interests and strengths well before Grade 9, not a rushed one in the final term.
  2. Keep foundations strong now. Because the placement score draws on school-based assessments across Grades 7 and 8, not just a single Grade 9 exam, steady revision through junior school matters more than a last-minute push. A patient tutor for the evening questions that come up along the way — in English or Kiswahili, strand by strand — helps keep those foundations solid without turning every term into exam pressure.

What is deliberately absent from that list is anything drastic: there is no need to switch schools, no need to buy an entirely new set of books, and no need to panic that your child has been studying "the wrong curriculum." CBC-aligned materials remain valid under CBE, because it is the same underlying design. The renaming is a reason to understand the system a little better, not a reason to act on fear.

Why the name confusion is everywhere

News coverage, school circulars, and learning apps currently mix CBC and CBE freely, and that is not a sign that something is wrong — it reflects a genuine transition period where both names refer to the same system. Using either name is fine in conversation with a teacher or another parent. What is worth checking is whether the specific materials, revision guides, or learning tools you are using are aligned to the current curriculum designs, rather than an outdated version from early in the CBC rollout. Common questions

Is CBC being scrapped in Kenya?

No. CBC has not been scrapped or replaced with something unrelated. Following the Presidential Working Party on Education Reform, the framework is now officially referred to as CBE — the same competency-based approach, reorganised around learning pathways, with a new name.

What does CBE stand for?

CBE stands for Competency Based Education. It is the official successor name for CBC (Competency Based Curriculum), following recommendations from the Presidential Working Party on Education Reform. The underlying approach — competencies over cramming, continuous assessment — is unchanged.

What are the three Senior School pathways?

STEM, Social Sciences, and Arts & Sports Science. Learners are organised into one of these three pathways at Senior School, Grade 10 to 12. The first Senior School Grade 10 cohort began in January 2026.

Did the subjects change from CBC to CBE?

The core approach did not change: learning areas, continuous assessment, and CBC-aligned materials remain valid. What changed is mainly structural — the name, and how Senior School is organised into the three pathways.

What exams does my child sit under CBE?

Two national checkpoints: KPSEA at the end of Grade 6, and KJSEA at the end of Grade 9. Both are run by KNEC. Beyond that, assessment is continuous and school-based, not one final make-or-break exam.
