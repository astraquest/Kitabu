Mathematics

"My child is failing maths." What’s really going on — and the calm way to fix it

Kitabu AI team · Updated 4 July 2026

Before you buy another revision book or book emergency tuition, it’s worth understanding what a failing maths grade usually means. It’s rarely what parents fear.

What a failing grade usually hides

Maths is the most cumulative subject on the timetable. Fractions feed decimals, decimals feed percentages, percentages feed ratio and rate — and by Grade 8, half the syllabus stands on foundations poured in Grades 5 and 6. When one of those foundations never set properly, every topic built on it wobbles. The mark says "failing Grade 8 maths." The truth is usually "missing two Grade 6 ideas."

This is different from how most other subjects work, which is exactly why it catches families off guard. A child can have a weak week in Social Studies and a strong week the next, with no lasting effect, because most topics in that subject don’t depend directly on each other. Maths doesn’t forgive that way. A gap left unrepaired doesn’t stay the same size — it quietly grows, because every new topic that leans on it inherits the wobble.

The foundation chain, concretely

It helps to see one real chain rather than take that claim on faith. Start at the bottom: counting teaches a child that numbers represent quantity. Place value teaches that where a digit sits changes what it’s worth — the difference between 24 and 42. Fractions teach that a whole can be split into equal parts and named by that split. Decimals are simply fractions written a different way, so a shaky fraction idea produces a shaky decimal idea. Percentages are decimals scaled to "out of 100" — another fraction in disguise. Ratio compares two quantities using exactly the same reasoning that underpins fractions in the first place.

Now take a typical Grade 8 ratio problem: sharing a sum of money between three people in the ratio 2:3:5. Solving it quietly requires a learner to find a total of parts (addition, but also a grouping idea from place value), express each person’s share as a fraction of the whole (fractions), and sometimes convert that share into a percentage to check it (percentages, decimals). A learner who never fully secured Grade 5–6 fraction concepts doesn’t fail this question because the ratio idea is hard — they fail it because the fraction machinery underneath the ratio idea was never built. The exam paper says "ratio." The real gap says "fractions, two years ago."

The same chain keeps extending upward. A Grade 9 problem about compound percentage change, or a Grade 10 problem comparing rates between two quantities, is built from exactly the same underlying ideas, just recombined and dressed in new vocabulary. This is precisely why a maths gap left alone for a year or two doesn’t stay the same size — it grows, silently, because every new topic added on top inherits the same wobble underneath it.

Three signs it’s a gap, not laziness

Parents often reach for "he’s not trying" before "she’s missing a foundation," because effort is what we can see and gaps are invisible. Three signs point more reliably at a gap:

  • The difficulty is specific to maths. A child who is engaged and capable in English, Science, or Social Studies but consistently struggles only in maths is showing a subject-specific gap, not a general attitude problem.
  • They can follow the new lesson in class, but fail mixed exercises. This is one of the clearest signals there is. A child who nods along when the teacher demonstrates one method, then falls apart on a worksheet mixing five methods together, isn’t failing to understand today’s lesson — they’re failing to draw on an older idea the mixed exercise assumes is already secure.
  • The same TYPE of question fails across different topics. If word problems fail whether they’re about fractions, ratio, or percentage, the common thread isn’t any one topic — it’s a skill underneath all of them, often reading and translating a real-world situation into a calculation.

None of these signs require a specialist to notice. They require watching one more layer down than the grade on the page — not just "did they pass," but "which questions, exactly, and what do those questions have in common." Most parents can spot the pattern within a week or two of paying attention this way.

Why trying harder doesn’t work

More hours on this term’s topics can’t fix last year’s gap — it’s like painting a wall that isn’t plumb. Worse, the child concludes they’re "just bad at maths," stops asking questions in class to avoid embarrassment, and the gap compounds quietly. The single most valuable thing you can do is separate effort from diagnosis: the problem isn’t how hard they’re working, it’s where they’re working.

This mislabelling has a real cost beyond the mark itself. A child who has decided they are "just bad at maths" starts avoiding the subject wherever avoidance is possible — sitting quietly instead of asking a clarifying question, guessing instead of showing working, choosing electives later on that route around it. The identity forms faster than most parents expect, and unwinding "I’m bad at maths" tends to take longer than fixing the actual gap that caused it. That is one more reason diagnosis, not extra effort, is the first move.

What a good diagnostic actually asks

A proper maths check-up doesn’t start with this term’s syllabus. It starts by walking downward — asking simple, calm questions from progressively earlier grade levels until it finds the point where the child answers confidently and consistently. That point, wherever it lands, is solid ground. Everything above it is where the repair work should focus; everything at or below it can be left alone.

This is why the framing matters as much as the questions. A check-up that feels like a punishment test — timed, graded, compared to classmates — makes a child perform worse out of anxiety and defensiveness, which corrupts the very thing you’re trying to measure. A check-up that feels like a curious, judgment-free conversation gets an honest picture of what the child actually knows. Say this to your child before any diagnostic, at home or otherwise: "This isn’t a test you can fail. It’s just us finding out what you already know for sure, so we stop wasting time on things that aren’t actually the problem." That single sentence changes how a child approaches the questions — with curiosity instead of dread — and a relaxed child gives you a far more accurate map of where the real gap sits.

The calm fix, step by step

  • 1. Find the real starting point. A proper diagnostic — not a test that punishes, a check-up that locates. Kitabu’s free ≈15-minute check-up maps exactly which strands are solid and which are missing, even when they belong to an earlier grade.
  • 2. Repair below, quietly. The child revises the missing Grade 6 ideas in private, at their own pace, with no classmates watching. This is where a patient tutor matters: ask again, and again, no judgment.
  • 3. Reconnect to classwork. As foundations set, this term’s topics start making sense on their own — often faster than parents expect.
  • 4. Watch the climb, weekly. Progress you can see is progress that continues. A Friday message saying "improved from 42% to 68% on decimals" changes the mood of a whole household.

A term-recovery picture

It helps to know roughly what repair looks like as it unfolds, so you don’t give up in week two expecting a week-eight result. In the first week or two, the work is mostly invisible from the outside — quiet repetition of foundational ideas, often below the current grade, with no dramatic change in classwork yet. Around weeks three to six, the reconnection usually starts: mixed exercises that used to stall begin to move, and a child starts finishing homework in the ordinary amount of time rather than the extended standoff you’d grown used to. By week seven and beyond, for most learners, that reconnection has become durable — the foundation holds under a new topic being layered on top, and the maths conversation at home shifts from "why can’t you" to genuinely watching real improvement, week by week.

What not to do while repair is happening

A few common instincts slow this process down, even when they come from genuine concern. Piling on extra worksheets from this term’s topic doesn’t help if the gap sits two grades below it — it’s more practice on a foundation that still isn’t there. Comparing progress to a sibling or classmate ignores that every gap is different in size and location, and comparison adds pressure without adding information. And switching diagnostic methods every few weeks — a tutor this month, an app the next, a different tutor after that — never lets any single approach finish mapping the gap before starting over. Consistency, even boring consistency, is what lets repair actually complete.

Common questions

Why is my child failing maths but passing other subjects?

Maths is unusually cumulative — each topic depends on the one before it, more than in most other subjects. A single missing foundation from an earlier grade can quietly sink every topic built on top of it, while subjects that don’t stack the same way stay unaffected.

Is my child just bad at maths?

Almost never. What looks like "bad at maths" is usually one or two specific missing ideas from an earlier grade. Once those are found and repaired, most learners reconnect with classwork faster than parents expect.

Should I hire a maths tutor or use a learning app?

Either can work if it starts by diagnosing the actual gap rather than repeating this term’s topics louder. A calm, non-judgmental check-up that maps solid and shaky strands — whether from a tutor or an app — matters more than the format it comes in.

How long does it take to fix a maths gap?

It depends on how deep the gap runs, but many families see the first signs of reconnection within a few weeks of consistent, focused repair — not overnight, and rarely in a single sitting.

What is a maths diagnostic, exactly?

It’s a check-up, not a test. Instead of grading this term’s work, it walks down through earlier grade levels asking simple questions until it finds the point where the child is completely solid — that point is where repair should start.
