Homework

CBC homework is confusing parents — here’s how to actually help your child

Kitabu AI team · Updated 4 July 2026

The methods are new, the terms are new, and the worksheet looks nothing like what we studied. Here’s what actually helps — and none of it requires becoming a CBC expert.

First, the truth: it’s not you

The CBC teaches differently on purpose. Where we memorised, today’s learners are asked to show working, apply ideas to real situations, and explain their thinking. That’s a better way to learn — and a disorienting thing to supervise when the method on the page isn’t the one you were taught. Almost every Kenyan parent of a Grade 4–10 learner is having this exact evening.

It helps to say that plainly, out loud, in your own house: you are not failing your child by not knowing this. The curriculum changed. You didn’t get a manual. What follows is the manual — the short version, built for tonight’s worksheet, not for a teaching certificate.

It also helps to remember what homework is actually for under CBC. It isn’t a grade-collecting exercise handed to a stranger to mark in isolation — it’s meant to be a visible trail of thinking that a teacher can use to see exactly where a class, and a child, need more support. That changes what "good help" looks like. Your job most evenings isn’t to produce a perfect worksheet; it’s to protect an honest one, mistakes included, because an honest mistake is information the teacher can use next week.

What helps (and doesn’t need expertise)

  • Ask "show me how your teacher did it" instead of teaching your way. Two competing methods confuse a child. Let them reconstruct the class method — the reconstruction itself is revision.
  • Praise the working, not just the answer. CBC marks reward the process. A child who shows clear working with one slip is closer than a child with a lucky answer.
  • Shrink the session. Twenty focused minutes beats a two-hour standoff. Stop while it’s still going well.
  • Keep a "stuck list." When neither of you can crack a question, write it down and move on. A list of specific stuck topics is gold — for the teacher, and for revision time.
  • Never do the homework for them. A finished worksheet the child can’t explain just hides the gap until exam time.

A worked example: "show me how your teacher did it"

It helps to see the technique in action, because it feels strange the first time you try it. Here’s a realistic three-line exchange over a fractions worksheet — the kind that plays out in thousands of Kenyan sitting rooms most weeknights.

Parent: "I don’t remember fractions being taught like this. Show me how your teacher explained it on the board."
Child: "She said we draw the whole thing first, then split it into equal parts, then shade the parts we’re using — like a chapati cut into pieces."
Parent: "Okay, draw that for this question, the way she showed you. I’ll watch."

Notice what didn’t happen. The parent didn’t explain fractions using bar models, or the method they learned at school in the 1990s, or an approach borrowed from a YouTube video the night before. They asked the child to retrieve the classroom method from memory and perform it out loud. That retrieval — not the parent’s explanation — is the revision. If the child gets stuck partway through the drawing, that stuck point is now precisely located, which is worth more than a right answer arrived at by guessing.

Subject-by-subject quick tips

The "show me how your teacher did it" approach works everywhere, but each subject has its own small twist worth knowing.

  • Mathematics: praise the working, not the final answer. Ask to see every step on paper, even the ones that feel obvious to an adult. A wrong answer with clean, visible working is a stronger sign of learning than a correct answer with no working shown at all.
  • English and Kiswahili: ask your child to read their answer aloud before calling it finished. Composition and comprehension answers that sound wrong when spoken usually are wrong — awkward phrasing reveals itself the moment it leaves the mouth, in a way it never does sitting quietly on paper.
  • Integrated Science: connect the concept to something physical in the house. Evaporation is the wet floor drying after mopping; a lever is the bottle opener in the kitchen drawer. CBC science rewards learners who can apply ideas to real situations, so a five-second household example does real revision work.
  • Social Studies and Creative Arts: for projects, let your child explain the project brief to you in their own words before they start. If they can’t explain what is actually being asked, the project will drift off-brief no matter how much effort goes in — and that explanation is also good practice for how CBC expects learners to communicate their thinking.

Mistakes to avoid

A handful of habits quietly make homework harder for everyone, even when they come from good intentions. Watch for these:

  • Teaching your own method over the classroom method. It feels helpful, but a child now has two competing systems in their head at exam time, and confusion, not clarity, is what tends to show up on the page.
  • Letting homework run past the point of learning. An hour past frustration doesn’t add revision value — it usually erodes what was learned in the first twenty minutes, and it teaches a child to dread the whole subject.
  • Treating every wrong answer as a crisis. A worksheet is a snapshot of one evening, not a verdict on ability. Reacting to a single bad night the same way you would to a term of results sends the wrong signal about what actually matters.
  • Comparing siblings or classmates out loud. "Your sister never struggled with this" doesn’t motivate — it just adds shame to an already frustrating evening, which makes the child less likely to ask for help next time.

The 20-minute evening routine

Families who feel calmest about homework tend to run something close to this routine, most nights, without much ceremony:

  1. Settle. Two minutes of nothing but sitting down, books out, phone away. Don’t start the academic conversation until the fidgeting has stopped.
  2. One subject. Pick the subject with homework due soonest, not the one either of you enjoys most. Trying to cover everything most nights is how sessions become two-hour standoffs.
  3. Build the stuck list. Work through the questions. Anything that resists two honest attempts goes on the list and you both move on immediately — no lingering.
  4. Stop while it’s going well. The moment the child has had one clear success, that’s the exit point. Ending on a win is what makes tomorrow’s session easier to start.
  5. Two-minute teach-back. Before books close, ask your child to explain the one thing they’re now sure of, in their own words, in under two minutes. If they can teach it, it has landed.

When you’re both stuck

This is the moment families usually reach for expensive tuition — or give up. It’s also exactly the gap a patient tutor in the phone can fill: your child asks the question, gets it explained step by step the CBC way, in English or Kiswahili, and can ask again as many times as they need. Then a short practice quiz confirms it landed. What to do when you are the one who is lost

It is completely normal for a parent to be more lost than the child on a modern CBC worksheet — the methods, vocabulary, and even the subject names have changed since most of us sat exams. The stuck list isn’t only for your child’s benefit; it is for yours too. Write down what confused you as an adult in the same way you’d write down what confused your child, and treat both entries the same way: as things to hand to someone who explains patiently, rather than things to feel bad about.

This is precisely where a patient tutor in the phone earns its place in the evening routine — not to replace you, but to be the person in the room who never runs out of patience for a fifth explanation of the same idea, for either of you.

What to do when there’s no adult free at all

Not every household has a parent free at 6 PM, and that is worth planning for honestly rather than feeling guilty about. An older sibling can run the same "show me how your teacher did it" routine just as well as a parent — the technique works because of the question, not because of who is asking it. On evenings when nobody is available at all, the stuck list still does its job on its own: a child who writes down exactly where they got stuck, even alone, has done something more useful than staring at an unsolvable question for twenty minutes. The list becomes the conversation starter for whoever is free next — a parent later that night, a teacher the next morning, or a patient tutor in the phone in the meantime.

Watch the pattern, not the night

One hard evening means nothing. The same topic being hard for three weeks means something. That’s why weekly progress signals matter more than any single homework battle: they tell you where to point your energy while there’s still time to act.

Common questions

Do I need to understand CBC to help my child with homework?

No. The most useful thing a parent can do is ask "show me how your teacher did it" and listen, not teach a different method. Understanding the content matters far less than protecting the child’s confidence and habits.

How long should homework take in Grade 4 to Grade 10?

It varies by school, but a focused twenty-minute session per subject is a reasonable evening target for most Grade 4–10 learners. If homework is regularly taking hours, that is worth raising with the teacher, not just pushing through.

Should I correct my child’s homework before it’s submitted?

Gently, and only after they have tried. Point at a line and ask "are you sure about this one?" rather than fixing it yourself. A corrected-by-parent worksheet hides gaps that only show up later, at exam time.

What if we’re both stuck on the same question?

That is normal and not a failure. Write the question on the stuck list, move on, and let the teacher or a patient tutor in the phone fill the gap later. Two people staring at one impossible question rarely helps either of them.

Is it okay to use a learning app for homework help?

Yes, provided it explains rather than hands over answers. A good app walks the child through the CBC method step by step and checks understanding with practice questions — the goal is a child who can explain the answer, not just submit one.
