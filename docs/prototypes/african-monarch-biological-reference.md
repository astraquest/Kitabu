# African Monarch biological reference

Reference for the interactive specimen prototype. The subject is the adult African Monarch / African Queen / Plain Tiger, *Danaus chrysippus* (family Nymphalidae). This copy is intentionally short enough for hotspot cards.

## Reference appearance

Use a dorsal (upper-side), wings-open adult as the default teaching view. A well-supported reference form has a deep orange ground colour; dark-brown to black forewing leading edges, tips, and outer margins; four prominent white subapical forewing spots with smaller nearby spots; and a dark hindwing border containing a row of white spots. The hindwing also bears three dark spots near the end of the discal cell. A Kenyan National Museums field guide gives a typical wingspan of 65-75 mm.

Do not label this pattern as the only appearance of the species. It is one biologically valid reference form within a highly variable species.

## Hotspot copy

### Whole butterfly - An insect in three sections

**Display copy:** The adult butterfly has three main body sections: the head, thorax, and abdomen. The head carries its principal sense organs and mouthparts. Its wings and six legs attach to the thorax, while much of the digestive, breathing, and reproductive systems lies in the abdomen.

**Accuracy note:** Keep head, thorax, and abdomen visibly distinct in the model. The two wing pairs and all three leg pairs originate from the thorax, not the abdomen.

### Forewing - The forward wing pair

**Display copy:** The forewings are the upper, forward pair. In this reference form, each has an orange ground colour and a broad dark tip containing conspicuous white spots. The forewing pattern helps form the butterfly's warning display.

**Accuracy note:** The black-tipped pattern is variable in *D. chrysippus*; some morphs lack it. Avoid presenting the dark tip as a feature shared by every individual.

### Hindwing - The rear wing pair

**Display copy:** The hindwings are the lower, more rounded pair. In this reference form they are orange with a dark outer border, a row of pale spots, and dark spots near the central wing cell. They work with the forewings as a coupled flight surface.

**Accuracy note:** Hindwing ground colour is a major polymorphic trait: it may be orange/brown or include extensive white, depending on morph.

### Antenna - Chemical sensor

**Display copy:** Each club-tipped antenna is covered with microscopic sensory structures. These help the butterfly detect airborne chemicals, including odours involved in finding food and communicating with other butterflies.

**Accuracy note:** Model two slender, segmented antennae ending in distinct clubs. Do not render moth-like feathery antennae.

### Proboscis - A coiling drinking tube

**Display copy:** The proboscis is a long, straw-like mouthpart used to take in nectar and other liquids. It coils beneath the head when it is not being used.

**Accuracy note:** The resting pose should show a compact coil near the mouth, not a rigid projecting beak.

### Compound eye - Many visual units

**Display copy:** Each compound eye is made of many light-sensitive units called ommatidia. Together they are especially effective at detecting movement and colour; butterflies can also see ultraviolet wavelengths that humans cannot.

**Accuracy note:** Show one large compound eye on each side of the head. A fine faceted texture is appropriate, but oversized honeycomb facets would be diagrammatic rather than life-sized.

### Thorax - The movement centre

**Display copy:** The thorax is the middle body section. It anchors both pairs of wings and all six legs and contains the muscles that power flight and walking.

**Accuracy note:** Wing pivots and leg roots must connect to the thorax. This is also the correct centre for the prototype's wing-flap rig.

### Abdomen - The segmented rear body

**Display copy:** The abdomen is the long, segmented rear section. It contains much of the digestive system, breathing tubes and openings, and reproductive organs.

**Accuracy note:** Keep the abdomen segmented and narrower than the thorax; do not attach wings or legs to it.

### Legs - Six legs, four used for walking

**Display copy:** Like other insects, the African Monarch has six legs. Because it belongs to the brush-footed butterfly family, its front pair is reduced and held close to the body; it normally walks and perches on the other four.

**Accuracy note:** Include the small forelegs even if they are easy to miss. A model with only four total legs would be anatomically wrong.

### Wing scales - Colour in microscopic tiles

**Display copy:** Thousands of overlapping scales cover the wings. Pigments and microscopic scale structures create the visible colours and patterns, much like tiny pixels. Individual scales can detach, which may help a butterfly pull free from a spider web.

**Accuracy note:** Suggest scales with subtle surface texture or normal mapping. Large feather-like tiles would distort their true microscopic scale.

## Morph and interpretation caveats

- *Danaus chrysippus* is not represented by one universal orange-and-black pattern. A global analysis of 17,094 records found extensive polymorphism in three prominent traits: orange versus white hindwing, light orange versus dark brown background, and presence versus absence of the black forewing tip.
- East Africa contains a broad zone where several named morphs and intermediate patterns overlap. A Kenyan learner may encounter an individual that looks different from the prototype and still be seeing the same species.
- Treat the render as "one reference form of *Danaus chrysippus*," not as a diagnostic key. Identification from a single generated image should not be implied.
- Keep the dorsal and ventral surfaces distinct if an underside is later added. This first reference describes the dorsal, wings-open view.
- Do not infer sex from the generic reference pattern alone. Sex-specific scent structures require closer inspection and are outside the first prototype's hotspot scope.
